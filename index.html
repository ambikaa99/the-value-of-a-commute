A speculation

Have you ever thought about the value of your commute?

Today, transportation systems measure minutes traveled. This speculation explores what might change if we also recognized the recovery, connection, movement, and reflection that happen along the way.

Scroll to begin
How we got here
We built transportation systems around saving time.

Today, most mobility systems help us answer a single question:

“What's the fastest way to get there?”

Beyond travel time
But a journey is more than travel time.

People described commuting as time to:

Recover · Prepare · Connect · Reflect

Yet these forms of value rarely appear when we make transportation decisions.

The hidden trade-offs
So some choices remain invisible.

A route that takes five minutes longer might also:

help you move more
reduce stress
create moments of reflection
contribute to fewer cars on the road
But, what if we made them visible? What would it mean for the individual? What would it mean for the collective?
This speculation explores a simple question
Could future mobility systems help people recognize the value of choosing differently?

If individual choices changed, what could cities start seeing differently?

Small shifts in how people travel can add up to measurable changes across congestion, transit use, emissions, and public health.

The value of a street depends on what it enables.

The same corridor can move the same number of people while creating entirely different experiences for a city. What should transportation infrastructure optimize for beyond vehicle throughput?

The value of today's decisions compounds over time.

Transportation investments often shape behavior for decades. How might policymakers evaluate decisions not only through immediate outcomes, but through the futures they help create?

Maybe the future of mobility is not about getting somewhere faster.

Maybe it is about:

  • arriving calmer,
  • protecting attention,
  • reducing invisible stress,
  • and creating time that gives something back.

Because time was never neutral.
We just built systems that treated it that way.
